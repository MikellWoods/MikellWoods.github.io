[GENERAL ADVICE]

1. Is The Story A Right Fit For You?

the meaning of this question is for you to genuinely consider if you are the right person to be telling this sort of story.

In many cases whenever you're writing a plot it usually should have some sort of personal connection to you, so writing is much easier, but also comes from a personal experience making it more accurate and realistic. This question is important in knowing if you actually know enough information about this topic. In some cases, if you don't really know a lot about how detective work is done, you should probably do some research before you start assuming and writing your story. If you're going to make science fiction that's meant to be very grounded, you have to be well-versed in scientific inquiries more than the average person in order to make something that makes sense and isn't put together with no understanding. Many people are willing to suspend disbelief for stories, a lot of the time they'll completely allow the impossible, but not the improbable. The moment you crossed the line for suspending disbelief is when you make something that is very commonplace, or in other words, common sense, and don't use it. Completely ignoring blatant instincts and truths that should be known to the commons then having the story suffer from that. In the eyes of the public that is not only improbable, but it's socially and inconsequentially stupid for such a direct lack of general understanding. Improbable and impossible, double negatives. There is a lot of nuance that comes with this, so take much with a grain of salt, but really do try your best when it comes to researching your topics. You'll never have pinpoint accuracy, especially if you don't live in that kind of world so don't let other people get in your head when you make a mistake or try to make it as real and flushed out as possible. As long as you do a good job at representation, at the very least common people can see the difference between a well-crafted story being nitpicked and then genuine criticism from the masses.

2. Achievable Goals Due Time

having all the goals that you want to achieve can help you make a planned schedule to complete everything and come up with backups in case they fail.

It all depends if on if you work well with goals, if not. You don't have to do so, just an idea.

3. Set Your Personal Rules

this will be the foundation of creating your own personal style that comes to your writing and creative work.

It will show your morality and preferences with consistency throughout your art that ultimately represents you.

4. Make What You Want

while obvious advice, making something you're passionate about for whatever personal reasons those may be will make the creative process much easier and less of a challenge.

The love of making it will bleed through and be seen by your audience.

5. Old Ideas Become New With You

most new ideas are really just old ideas prepared in a different way, with a different perspective.

So you don't have to make something completely original, you just have a premise, prepare it in a style that you can do, replicate, then take old successful ideas, and give them a twist that is your signature unique style. Just like that, a new story has been born.

6. Save All Knowledge

when any new piece of information or random idea comes your way, make sure to catalog it for future use.

You can always go back to those random pieces of info and connect them with other ideas you've saved to make a new idea that never came naturally, but instead over time.

7. Non-Conformity Is Originality

subconsciously being in a nonlinear or different thinking sparks more creativity and "out-of-the-box" motivation.

It can be especially beneficial in the scenario where you wish for something that is created to have a sense of authenticity and originality. If you're in a "copy-and-paste" or "follow-the-lead" environment, you're going to be pressured into doing something similar. Being in an area where you subvert that expectation and are reminded that you can be deeply rewarded for not conforming will continue the proper prospect of saving your creative vision in life and writing. Your vision of history is irreplaceable.

8. Casual Contemplation

anytime you force the activity of brainstorming makes walls appear and stops progressive thinking while actively wanting it.

For as frustrating as it always is pressuring yourself because of deadlines or simply because you wish to be in a mood we are not machines we have to simply go at the pace our mind is willing to comprehend. Take a step back and prepare for something different.

9. Mundane Task = Daydreaming

basic activities naturally lead your mind to wander and daydream, forming casual moments of brainstorming.

You may be actively trying to do other things to get away from frustrating activities, but there's nothing wrong with getting what you need out of the way. Not only does it clear your mind, but it also gets rid of things that might stop you from being able to continue working on your stories later, so you can continue working sooner.

10. Routine Build Stability

this may not be the case for everyone, but it could be useful in certain personal scenarios to have a sort of schedule.

Whether that be starting at a specific time, doing a certain activity, or being in a specific environment that allows you to be the most comfortable. This would require a lot of testing in order to find the best case and scenario, but it's worth a shot to see if you are more formulaic or more chaotic when it comes to your processes.

11. Attempt Restrictive Writing

it's hard honing in on something in a void, being in a metaphorically closed world helps creative thinking fill up your writing.

You shouldn't restrict all of your writing, but it is a good method of seeing what you're capable of and possibly find unique ways to get around your scenario's rules and do something unexpected.

12. Have Rules For Each Story

it forces you to creatively get around rules themselves allowing for more possibilities and "out-of-the-box" thinking.

This ensures your plot choices won't seem spineless, half-baked, and/or convenient since the rules must seriously be applied and followed. Just be human and use the rules to your advantage.

13. Many Paths Leave You Lost

when we wish to create, it could come from the fact that we want something, but don't exactly know what we want.

Just like when we're hungry, but don't know which place to go, we are stuck at a crossroads with too many options. Some people can thrive or suffer when with too many options, if you are someone that suffers try to limit many options and be more restrictive with your intake. Allowing you to stop bargaining with yourself and start deciding.

14. Dig A New Way

if you're working on a story and you've been stuck on it for a while but still in the mood to keep writing, change focus.

Stepping back and working on a previous project or something completely new is a way to turn different gears in your head and relax from the ensuing frustration of being stuck. Then you can later return in a better mood and possibly with new ideas.

15. Problem Solving Is Creative

break the chain of being stopped with infinite possibilities and give yourself something distinct to focus on: a problem.

There are most definitely moments in our life where we want something and we feel like other people wanted, but it isn't necessarily discussed. I'd recommend trying to switch gears from what is the most creative to what solves a problem. A good way of writing a story is having a mechanic that solves a problem and then making that problem-solving mechanic creative.

16. Incorporate Your Life View

possessing and conjuring your uniquely opinionated and personalized life leads you to thinking in a way no one else could replicate.

Take your individual moments and real-world experiences as a benchmark to set up a truly unique story.

17. Make It Personally Unforgettable

if you can personally forget about a story or even writing in general, then it's best to hold off on working on it.

You should focus on a story that is hard to get away from or that is calling to you. If you focus on a story that you're clearly not passionate about at that very moment then you're going to be getting frustrated. When it starts to feel sluggish it becomes a chore, so focus on what is exciting right now.

18. Inspiration Needs Strong Base

inspiration comes from doing work in the first place, not before anything has started.

Always try to remember when in a sort of "flow-state" where concepts and scenarios just seem to be fluently coming to you, you're going to have to put in delicate and strong thinking at the forefront when it comes to making anything digestible and entertaining.

19. All In Preparation

most ideas that come from you are from previously established knowledge in reality or ideas relayed from other work.

So when being inspired and creating something new from previous works, instead of thinking you MADE the idea, think about how through your unique way of writing you're going to prepare a fresh way of cooking, preparing, and presenting multiple ideas through a unique lens that no one has ever lived through and could never replicate.

20. Progression Moves In Silence

very commonly you will try to create something and get no feedback from anything, which can be very demoralizing.

The more you make and release, the better you get. Which will slowly document your progress and your resume. If anything, your work is going to only get better and better. Do remember if you feel cringe from your old work, then that means you have improved and learned. Good job.

21. A Portfolio Needs Beginning

make sure the book you first release is something more simple; save the hyper-complex concepts for later.

You can work on other stories that are way more complex, but it's better to wait and flush those out over a long period rather than it being your first. It's a good idea to have your book be in the realm of something that has been made before, but with elements that haven't been made. A purely unknown book is bound to have an unknown audience.

22. Brand Yourself

make sure to build a portfolio and establish your identity when it comes to publishing and what service you provide.

Know your specific creative genre and explicitly understand what target audience you want to bring into your space.

23. Perfect Your Pitch

once you have a clear and concise idea, try to condense it down to just one or two sentences.

Pack in as much core information as possible while presenting it in the most intriguing, hook-filled way imaginable.

24. Compare Similar Media

figure out what most closely relates to your current idea by evaluating multiple tracking examples.

A great way to identify a genre, style, and audience for your concept is to find the factors that are similar to them, but all put together uniquely in your one story.

25. Know Your Intriguing Factors

make sure you have highly important and identifiable characteristics about your story that sets it apart.

Something that readers will not only be intrigued by once it's mentioned but people will gravel to it almost every time they mention the story. Having a specific fact or something completely unique is the key part of becoming memorable compared to hundreds of standard, well-written stories.

[CHARACTERS]

26. Embody/Empathize With Them

a good exercise in feeling and breathing the life into a character is by embodying them fully.

By responding to your own hypothetical situations while in their head could help flesh out the character and make you realize things that feel very natural for the character to respond with that could possibly surprise what you originally thought you wanted them to be. Sometimes casually and smoothly walking is the fastest way to close the distance.

27. Possess Desperation

when making a character it's quite important to know what the character is truly desperate for.

Knowing so truly evolves the character's nature more for them to have an overwhelming sense of need for something that could possibly be as simple as a want they could live without, but they don't see it that way. By figuring out what they are desperate for in life and how much they're willing to give up to achieve it will speak loudly where their morality lies.

28. Desires That Bring Peace

while desperation can be seen as a survival need, desire will be used in the case for their internal wants.

By knowing their wants it would be casually assumed that by possessing this desire will lead to them having peace even if specifically their peace is chaos. Having a character know what they long for, but still possibly understands they may never get it helps build more integrity and personality, making them feel human.

29. Devotion Of Their Will

people naturally possess a sense of devotion to something in their life one way or another.

This could be as simple as the need to survive, or much more complex like religion, family values, societal traditions, or personal moral agendas. This will tell the type of person your character is expected to be and how they handle threats. Consider the possible insanity and delirium that comes with deep devotion.

30. Senses Of Delirium

no one is omniscient; we all have our own levels of personal delirium due to our hyper-opinionated perspective.

If you make all characters flatly grounded in "reality's truth", they will simply be filled with your opinion. You can write an all-around healthy, normal character, but if there's no unique philosophy behind them, they might feel like a psychopathic facade trying to manipulate people into thinking they're normal. Use this deliberately, or reconsider it.

31. Atypical Complexity Is Real

humans are complex in almost every sense, so if you want your characters to feel real, make them feel lived in.

Make their life hardy and plentiful, with short-term and long-term goals, faith and wishes. Riddle your character with perspective and unfold them over time. People can start to develop a fascination with them because they relate, or because they're so unbelievably alien in the way they react to life that they're impossible to look away from.

32. Make Them Lie

when introducing a character you can have people or the character themselves actively lie about who or what they are.

Whether they're lying to the audience and then slowly reveal it to be false over time or lying to other characters. It lets a mystery linger over and shows that they can be conniving and they have more to do than simply what is displayed. Just keep it consistent and authentic to the character.

33. Intelligence Learns From Mistakes

people are not omnipotent, they do not know everything and thus are not always correct, even if they are the smartest.

If you're going to have a character that needs to be smart you need to show they can fail and most importantly that they learn from their failure. Do not make them impervious to failure. Then all you're doing is making them impervious to consequences which shows heavy narrative bias and severe plot armor troubles.

34. Creatively Powerful Characters

give your protagonist and antagonist more thought-out structural advantages instead of unforeseen power upgrades.

It could be as simple as knowing the layout of a land that everyone else is completely unfamiliar with, or a lack of social constraints allowing no care for repercussions. Just always make sure that it connects natively with their established personality and backstory without causing logical plot holes.

35. Make Them Entertaining

the 4 main keys of doing it is to have them either be likable, relatable, unique, or purely entertaining.

Having a character be simply likable due to their good actions is an obvious way to go, but characters do not inherently need to be likable people to be incredibly entertaining to watch. Villains are a prime example of this through the relatability of their raw actions or the absolute uniqueness of their underlying struggles.

36. Demonstrate With Uncertainty

there will always be a prime time for characters to demonstrate cards or abilities they've hidden until the right moment.

Not only is it key in making antagonists imposing by knowing they possess the unknown, but it works for your protagonists too. Make sure the ability or knowledge they possess is reasonable to their background and that there is a logical reason why they chose to withhold or show off their cards here. Keep your cards in a clean row.

37. People Contradict Themselves

everyone contradicts themselves when they make exceptions for themselves or others based on their morality.

This doesn't have to be the case for every single character, but it is ultimately realistic that people will be a bit delusional when their beliefs clash with their immediate relationships. Make sure that the contradictions make sense for the character themselves. Ensure the characters contradict themselves, but make sure you don't.

38. Try A Desperate Antagonist

an enemy at equal levels, or even weaker, can be terrifying if they are miles more desperate than the hero.

Having your villain be reasonable for a while and could equally end out with either one of you winning but having the villain be more desperate willing to stoop lower than you is a sign of a messy war; they will grow unpredictable and unreasonable. It becomes a test on how low the protagonist is willing to stoop in order to win. Then it becomes an overarching question: what is the ultimate goal, victory or your identity? And if both the antagonist and protagonist goal is victory, then who really is the evil in the scenario? Implications are everything.

[DIALOGUE & INTERACTION]

39. Speaking Isn't The Beginning

having your character come alive through an action instead of blatantly reading off thoughts engages the audience.

By letting your audience feel the environment and context naturally, you trust their intelligence, and they will be incredibly grateful for that narrative respect.

40. Context And "Why" Of Dialogue

break communication down into a structural chain of subconscious thought for your cast.

You need to know what they desire, what they believe in, and how the conversation is going to benefit them and move the story. If a dialogue scene doesn't push the plot or get them closer to what they want, cut it. Map out what they want from the other person, their contingency plan if they fail, and why this conversation must happen now.

41. Subtext, The Said Vs The Means

on average, people are not robotic or blatantly honest when it comes to expressing their ultimate intentions.

Subtext is inject words with hidden contexts while still getting across the core meaning. People tiptoe around uncomfortable scenarios, shooting into a general direction without directly asking to go there. Using subtext is tremendously powerful, allowing you to imply an entire backstory or emotional state without detailed info-dumping.

42. Emotion, Reaction From Actions

invoke sadness or anger in a manner that is fundamentally empathetic to your surrounding readers.

If a character's loved one dies and they don't react at all, it feels completely apathetic and off-putting. Make the actions naturally empathetic situations. If a character reacts differently to an insult than they do to other events, the audience understands the weight. Make actions that cause proper reactions that allow the audience to empathize.

43. Need Empathy To Sympathy

in life, you need the character to be running through existence and being affected by scenarios we can actively empathize with.

Even if we don't fully understand or sympathize with the specific choice a character makes directly, it is about understanding the baseline emotional toll of the scenarios they are placed in. This makes it more empathetically motivated than sympathetically motivated.

44. Emotion Blooms Through Evolution

to make people empathize with your characters, they have to explicitly understand why the emotion is happening.

If the audience has no frame of reference for the character's past or mindset, it will seem as if they are reacting impulsively and unruly. Showing not only how personally it is affecting them but also the structural origins that come from it is tremendously important.

[WRITING TECHNIQUES]

45. Theme, The Question

when you want to make your story revolve around an idea it shouldn't be a single word, it should be a statement.

If your theme is a single topic, it has too many avenues to explore in one story. Making a specific statement keeps the story digestible and helps guide your writing by cleanly discarding unconnected subjects. If you want to explore a broad topic like Human Nature or Scientific Progress, make it a series rather than a single story.

46. Scenarios Don't Preach

it is very important to make sure that your theme doesn't seem like it's preaching a moral story to children.

That will be patronizing. It makes more sense to have a theme that has a philosophical thought that you wish to shed light on, but set it up in a scenario that explores the raw possibilities and natural results that come from those choices. This makes your point feel authentic rather than tone-deaf and performative.

47. Victory Is Strategy, No Silver Platter

make sure your character's success is not blind luck, because luck just means you allowed it as the writer.

You can't allow outside forces to give them a free win on a silver platter. If outside powers assist, ensure it requires the core crew to complete a dangerous job so the others can help. If they do nothing or fail and still win, it shows no consequences and can be seen as cowardice in terms of writing a story. Success must be earned.

48. Title Calls A Visual

your title must endure the passage of time, be instantly recognizable, and invoke deep curiosity.

It should acknowledge its identity throughout time, match the story's tone, and invoke questions and discussion. It's all a subjective endeavor so find that signature style of yours where at any point when someone looks at a title you made, they know it's yours.

49. Consequences Must Be Personal

realistically, there has to be something deeper than a broad "world-ending" stakes format.

Normally if you have a fantastical story, the world ending is pretty personal since that's their home, but it affects the whole population and becomes a common enemy. It's important to have elements that make the threat deeply personal to the character. Even if the enemy doesn't have a personal vendetta, have the protagonist make it personal.

50. The World Is A Character

make the environment and setting feel like another distinct person that characters must interact with.

They must respond to it, survive against it, and even talk to it in some instances. Keeping the place contained in a sense—even if that area is unbelievably large—can help the audience understand the scope of what you're trying to portray. Making an entire world respond to everything makes the story truly delectable.

51. Writing Is Unique And Non-uniform

just like how you don't live for everyone, you don't write for everyone either. Accept that reality completely.

There are going to be people that don't like the way you write, your style, or the stories you tell. That's just something you're going to have to live with. Continue doing what gives you purpose; your view on life and writing is irreplaceable. Do not let a few words stop your endless expression of authenticity.

52. Don't Play Safe, Stakes Are High

show that your story has true meaning; if heroes win without a scratch, what was the point of the journey?

When you make a story take itself seriously, it doesn't mean the threat has to be world-ending, it just means it's a legitimate threat that can and will hurt your characters. They must suffer consequences from choices and pure chance. An audience is much more engaged when they are anxious about characters they love being hurt.

53. Everything Has Consequences

consequences don't just mean bad punishments; mundane actions or good choices cause butterfly effects.

Having a character do something that comes to affect something later in the future shows that you care about little details. It rewards audience members that notice a subtle action that seems useless at the moment but returns at an even bigger point of reveal. It shows the audience that everything matters and shouldn't be put aside.

54. No Over Explaining

a key factor in having audiences view your story as being intelligent is not insulting their intelligence.

By not over-explaining and spelling things out for them, you show that you trust them, that you properly set the scene for them to solve it themselves, and that you are confident in your own writing skills. Just have faith in your audience; they will greatly appreciate it as long as you did your job right.

55. Signs Of Hidden Truths

leave breadcrumbs that not only your characters can discover to solve a mystery, but your audience can too.

Allowing yourself to give subtle signs that lead to the truth is a good way to avoid over-explaining things. By simply setting fragments of reality into your stories, readers can piece it together themselves without you having to make a whole paragraph explaining it straight to their face.

56. Didn't Know, But Should Have

when writing an effective twist event or subverting expectations, do not make it completely impossible.

Going overboard makes it nonsensical and idiotic. Instead, the way you want your audience to feel when the twist comes to fruition is that they didn't see it coming, but they realized they should have. This means you left breadcrumbs so well that it kept the mystery while being honest about where it was going. The perfect temperature for a twist.

57. Keep Promises

avoid empty promises that you just discard later, or you will risk completely disheartening your readers.

When setting out the details and the journey of your story there is bound to be some silent promises made. Like if your journey is all about getting a special magical object but as a twist it turns out it never existed, it's practically lying about your hook. A worse offense is changing genres partway through, like making a fantasy knights story turn into sci-fi time traveling. That will be unbelievably confusing and blatantly lying about the story you wanted to tell. Look out for these choices so you don't make empty promises. Whether your audience believes it's genius or cruel, they will remember it.

58. Keep The Genre Intact

it's a good habit to know everything you want to include so there is no possibility of derailing the original genre.

Being open with how you're wanting to present your concepts is what's going to show that you're honest and can be trusted. If you're going to promise a noir detective story with supernatural elements, but then they suddenly time travel from entering portals on different planets, clearly something is going out of line. It is possible to blend elements, but you must be upfront and honest about that early on. If things happen entirely out of nowhere, readers are not going to trust you. Give them what you promised.

59. Solve Centered Mysteries

when sprinkling ambiguity in your stories, you must pay close attention to exactly how much you're leaving out.

If you have a big mystery in the center of your story, you should definitely answer it in some capacity. Leaving it completely ambiguous will make it seem like you were lying, especially if the entire story revolved around finding that truth. If the story isn't about solving it, reveal early on that the mystery is closed and won't be pursued so people don't feel cheated. You can have an ending with ambiguity, but you have to give them something they can hold onto to even form an interpretation, otherwise they just wave the white flag.

60. No Time Wasting

avoid building up elaborate details or over-explaining mechanics when they never end up being important.

When you heavily build up details that never matter later, it feels like you're making a big, looming promise for something that never ends up delivering on the hype. It ultimately leaves the reader feeling highly disappointed, and if you fail bad enough, it can often become misplacingly comedic where the joke ends up being on you.

61. Hook Then Reward

the start is always about hooking your audience, and the ending is all of the payoff and reward that comes from finishing.

Keep in mind that the structural layout of your project relies heavily on these bookends. Make sure that your hook is not only highly satisfactory to get people engaged and wanting to see how it goes, but that the ending is entirely supplementary. It is the core execution of the story that makes the entire investment worth it to the audience.

62. Remove Redundant Scenes

if a scene or dialogue doesn't contribute to the next chain of events or push the plot, consider pulling it out.

Take it with a grain of salt because filler moments can allow you to build character development and deep connections. It's a good strategy to keep things moving like a tight chain, but there should still be room for downtime. Think of it as a meal: the elements that push the story forward are the protein, the world rules are the vegetables, and downtime is the starch that fills you up. Just make sure it always has a point.

63. Joy Amplifies Despair

use genuine moments of joy and happiness to starkly contrast with surrounding events of sadness.

A very popular saying is "if sadness didn't exist, we wouldn't know what happiness is." This is definitely relevant in fiction. Especially in situations where we have great moments that make us happy for characters connecting, and then later those connections failing in brutal moments makes the narrative impact much more upsetting. Showing them enjoying themselves blissfully brings to life how bad it can get compared to how good it was.

64. Genuine Wins And Fails

failing and winning is a huge factor in life, and utilizing both properly sets a realistic tone for your story.

By showing both sides of the coin naturally, you show your readers that they cannot simply expect the heroes to seamlessly win every single time. It directly establishes real-world weight, making it clear that there are going to be lingering consequences and that painful sacrifices genuinely have to be made along the path.

65. Try Blurring Lines

attempt to blur the line between good and evil, and take it further by blurring the line between winning and failing.

Moral ambiguity is a well-explored topic, but look at what point you consider winning or failing, or when no progress is made but things change anyway. Is change a win or a fail? If you go on a journey to find a magical item but lose your arm, an eye, and your friends, you got what you wanted but lost much more than you thought. Was it worth it, and does that truly conclude winning?

[HOW TO SAVE THE ENDING]

66. Nurture Your Ending

always make sure to have a good sense of pacing near the end; don't rush the execution of your resolution.

The beginning and the ending are the absolute most important factors for your project. If you fail the introduction, people will never continue reading, and if you fail the ending, it will never be forgotten due to the immense disappointment. Give yourself plenty of time to deeply think over your ending and map out how to go about it.

67. Don't End With No End In Sight

it's a bad idea to end your story abruptly in the middle with no explanations or clean resolutions.

This usually happens due to rushing or thinking with too much ambiguity. A good course of action is to make sure you have the definitive answers to your mysteries, even if you don't tell the audience yet. Answer some questions while leaving others open for discussion. Try writing from an omnipresent perspective to blueprint everything clearly so readers don't feel confused and betrayed.

68. No Undeserved Endings

characters shouldn't get happy endings and sacks of riches when they put no actual effort into the journey.

To make it feel like they deserved to win, they must experience struggle along the way and make sacrifices. They can get help, but they must build the bridge themselves. Even for sad endings, there needs to be a clear setup showing the story itself deserved that conclusion. You can't have a sad ending that is completely misplaced or mean-spirited for no narrative purpose. It must fit the tone.

69. Stop Predictable Endings

it is completely okay to play into traditional genre tropes, but rethink an ending when it becomes too predictable.

Not every single ending needs to have a wild, shocking twist that's explicitly meant to trick people, but it absolutely needs to be an ending that makes the reader feel something real. If the audience can easily predict exactly how the ending is going to go from miles away, they won't feel anything; they will just slowly deflate the whole time until they reach the final page.
